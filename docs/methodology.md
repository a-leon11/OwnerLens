# Methodology

This document explains how OwnerLens turns a registry into red flags, what each choice
means, and where the method stops being reliable.

## 1. Scope and responsible use

OwnerLens is a due-diligence aid for **legal entities**: who owns and runs a company, and
whether the structure around it deserves a closer look. That is the same question asked in
vetting a supplier, a counterparty or a partner.

- The repository contains **no real data** and **no code that collects data** from any
  registry. Everything it analyses is generated by `generate.py`.
- The data model keeps personal data to a minimum on purpose: a person is an id and a
  name. There are no birth dates, no home addresses and no family links, and the tool has
  no way to represent them.
- A flag is an **indicator**. Every structure below has innocent explanations, and the
  tool cannot tell them apart. A flag means "read the primary documents", never "this
  company or person did something wrong".
- It is not a tool for building profiles of private individuals or families. If you use it
  on real data, work from sources you are lawfully allowed to use, keep to companies and
  public roles, and verify every flag against the original records before relying on it.

## 2. Data model

Five CSV files (see `registry.py`): people, companies, addresses, ownership stakes and
directors. An owner in `ownership.csv` can be a person (`P-...`) or a company (`C-...`),
so a stake of one company in another expresses a layer. The loader checks the data before
anything is analysed: unknown ids, a company owning itself, stakes outside 0 to 100,
owners adding up to more than 100%, duplicate stakes and companies dissolved before they
were incorporated are all rejected with a clear message.

## 3. Effective ownership

If person P owns 50% of company A and A owns 40% of company B, P effectively owns
50% x 40% = 20% of B. P may also hold B directly, or through other companies. Each way of
reaching B is a *route*, and the **effective stake is the sum of the routes**.

Worked example from the demo registry (`python -m ownerlens owners C-0007`):

```
P-0214 Pablo Walsh: 26.0%  <- beneficial owner
    16.0%  P-0214 > C-0007
     6.0%  P-0214 > C-0305 > C-0007       (60% of C-0305 x 10% of C-0007)
     4.0%  P-0214 > C-0016 > C-0007       (50% of C-0016 x  8% of C-0007)
```

Routes are found by climbing the ownership graph upwards from the company until a person
is reached. **A route may visit a company only once.** That keeps circular holdings from
looping forever, and it makes the answer well defined: with cross-holdings the "true"
effective ownership is ambiguous, so the share that is lost in a loop is reported as
*unresolved* instead of being guessed. Routes longer than ten companies are dropped and
also count as unresolved.

A **beneficial owner** is a person whose effective stake is at least 25%. That figure is
a commonly used cut-off in beneficial-ownership rules, but jurisdictions differ, so it is
a setting (`Config.ubo_threshold`).

## 4. The rules

| Code | Rule | Fires when | Why it is worth a look | Innocent explanation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | Circular ownership | companies own each other in a loop | nobody clearly controls the companies; hard to audit | cross-holdings inside an old industrial group |
| R2 | Layered ownership | a beneficial owner's *closest* route has 3 or more intermediary companies | each layer makes the real owner harder to see | tax or liability structuring in a large group |
| R3 | Mass registration address | 6 or more companies share an address | typical of shell factories | a corporate group at one head office, a virtual-office provider |
| R4 | Nominee-style director | one person directs 8 or more companies and owns under 5% of at least 75% of them | the director may be a front for the real owners | a professional director or company-secretary firm |
| R5 | Stake split to stay under the threshold | a person's total reaches 25%, yet every route is below 25% | splitting a holding keeps each part under the reporting line | a founder who also holds shares through a family company |
| R6 | Ownership that cannot be traced | 25% or more (15 points) or 50% or more (30 points) of a company cannot be traced to a person | the owner is unknown | incomplete registry data |
| R7 | Closed within a year | dissolved 365 days or fewer after incorporation | single-use companies | a project that was cancelled |

Using the closest route in R2 matters: if a person can also hold the company directly, the
layers are not hiding anything. R4 counts the share of *effective* ownership, so owning
through another company still counts as owning.

## 5. Scoring

Each rule has a fixed number of points (R1 30, R2 25, R3 20, R4 30, R5 25, R6 30 or 15,
R7 20). A company's score is the sum of its points, capped at 100. A score of 50 or more
is **high**, 25 to 49 is **medium** and anything lower is **low**.

The weights are simple on purpose. Anyone can read a score and see exactly which rules
produced it, and the CSV report lists the evidence for every flag. The weights are
illustrative judgement calls, not calibrated on real cases, and they are all in
`rules.py` (`RULES` and `Config`).

## 6. How detection is measured

The generator plants seven known structures (an ownership ring, a five-company chain, a
twelve-company shell factory at one address with one nominee director, a split stake and
four companies with untraceable ownership) and records them in a ground-truth file. It also
builds harmless lookalikes: a transparent corporate group at one address, a busy owner who
directs ten companies, a legitimate three-company chain and two companies that closed
early.

`python -m ownerlens evaluate` repeats this over many random backgrounds and reports:

- whether each planted structure received the rule it was planted for;
- how many companies that were **not** planted reach medium or high (false positives);
- how many harmless lookalikes reach medium or high.

**Read the recall figure with care.** The structures were written to match the rules, so
finding them shows that the generator and the rules agree, not that the method works on
real registries. The informative result is the other side: ordinary companies and
lookalikes do not rise above low. Real registries are messier than this one.

## 7. Limitations

- Synthetic data only. No claim is made about accuracy on real data.
- Only percentage stakes are modelled. Voting rights, trusts, nominee shareholders and
  bearer shares are outside the model.
- It works on a single snapshot. Changes of ownership over time are not tracked, apart
  from the incorporation and dissolution dates.
- There is no name matching: the same person recorded under two spellings is two people.
  Entity resolution is the obvious next step and is not implemented.
- Thresholds and weights are not calibrated.
- Circular holdings are handled conservatively (see section 3), not solved exactly.
